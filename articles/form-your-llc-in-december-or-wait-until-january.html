Form Your LLC in December, or Wait Until January?

Direct answer

There is a third option, and it is usually the right one: file the paperwork in December and set the LLC's effective date to January 1. In most states that is allowed. Your name is locked in today, and your first year — for state charges and for taxes — starts clean in January. Forming in December without a future effective date makes 2026 your first year, even if the business exists for only three days of it.

Every December, new founders ask the same question — and both obvious answers cost something. Form now and the calendar charges you for a year that barely happened. Wait, and you spend January doing paperwork while someone else can register the name you chose. Here is the full picture, and the option most people never hear about.

The December problem

The day your LLC becomes effective, it exists — for your state and for the IRS. A business that exists in 2026 has 2026 obligations: in many states the full first-year charge, and a first, short tax year with its own filing, even if it never sold a thing. Three days of existence can buy you a full year of paperwork.

The January problem

Waiting is not free either. Business names are first come, first served: the name you picked in December can be taken in January. And instead of starting the year selling, you start it filing — articles, EIN, bank account — while competitors who filed earlier are already operating.

The third option: file in December, born on January 1

Most states let you choose a future effective date when you file — you submit the articles in December and state that the LLC takes effect on January 1. The name is reserved the day you file, and the company's first year, for fees and taxes, begins in January. It is the standard move for December founders who know it exists.

It is not universal: Alaska, for example, does not allow a future effective date — its articles take effect on filing. Check your own state's rule before you count on the January 1 date; the check takes minutes on the Secretary of State's site.

California founders: 2027 changes the math

California charges LLCs an $800 annual tax, including the first year. Under SB 122, for taxable years beginning on or after January 1, 2027 and before January 1, 2030, that first-year tax drops to $400. A California LLC that starts in December 2026 pays the old $800 first-year tax; one whose first year begins January 1, 2027 pays $400. In California, the effective date is worth real money this year.

The decide-by checklist

  • You want a specific name → file in December; names do not wait.
  • Your state allows a future effective date → set it to January 1 and take both advantages.
  • Your state does not allow one → compare the cost of a short 2026 year against a January filing, and decide with the real numbers.
  • You are forming in California → a first year that begins in 2027 starts at $400 instead of $800 under SB 122.
  • You will actually start selling in December → form now; a real December of sales is worth more than a clean calendar.

Frequently asked questions

Should I form my LLC in December or wait until January?

Usually neither extreme: file in December and set a January 1 effective date, if your state allows it. Forming in December with no future date makes the current year your first year — first-year charges and a short first tax year, even for a few days of existence. Waiting until January risks losing your chosen name and spends your first month on paperwork.

What is a delayed effective date?

It is a future start date you choose when you file your articles: the state records your filing today — reserving your name — and the LLC legally begins on the date you picked, such as January 1. Most states allow it; Alaska is a verified exception, where articles take effect on filing. Always confirm your own state's rule before filing.

What does a December start cost me with the IRS?

Your tax year begins when the business starts, so a December formation creates a first, short tax year — with the filing obligations that go with it — even if the business had no sales. It is one of the hidden costs of a December start, and one more reason the January 1 effective date exists.

What changes in California in 2027?

Under SB 122, California's first-year LLC tax drops from $800 to $400 for taxable years beginning on or after January 1, 2027 and before January 1, 2030. An LLC whose first year begins in December 2026 pays $800 for that first year; one that begins January 1, 2027 pays $400.
