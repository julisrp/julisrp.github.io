Registered Agent: What It Actually Is, and When You Can Be Your Own

Direct answer

A registered agent is the person or company the state and the courts use to deliver legal papers to your LLC: lawsuits and official notices go to the agent's address, not to your inbox. Every LLC must have one, in the state where it was formed, with a real physical street address — no P.O. box — and someone available during business hours. Yes, you can be your own agent if you live in that state, you have that address, and you are there during business hours. What almost nobody tells you: your address becomes public record, and if the papers arrive on a day you are not there, the case can move on without you.

It is the box everyone fills in without understanding it. When you form an LLC, the form asks for a name and an address under the heading 'registered agent,' and most people put down the first thing that comes to mind — their own name, their home address — without knowing what they are signing up for. That choice looks free. It has two hidden prices, and this article puts them on the table: what an agent actually is, when you can be your own, and when it pays to hire a company.

What a registered agent is, plainly

The registered agent is your company's legal address in the eyes of the state and the courts. If someone sues your LLC, the lawsuit papers do not arrive by email or text: a process server delivers them in person at your agent's address. Official state notices go there too — annual-report reminders, state tax notices, compliance warnings. That is why every state's law requires you to designate an agent when you form the LLC and to maintain one continuously: the state always needs a physical address where your company can be officially reached. It is not decorative paperwork on the form; it is how the legal system finds your business.

The three conditions for being your own agent

The law lets you be your own registered agent, and for a local business it is a legitimate choice — if you meet all three conditions. First: you live in the same state where you formed your LLC. Second: you have a real physical address in that state — a street address, not a P.O. box — because the papers are delivered in person. Third: you are at that address during business hours, available to receive them. If you work from home in the same state and your schedule is steady, the three conditions almost take care of themselves. If one of them fails, being your own agent stops being a saving and becomes a risk.

The two costs that don't show up in the price

Being your own agent costs no money. It costs two other things. The first is your privacy: your agent's name and address are public record, open to anyone. Put your home address down, and anyone can look it up in the state's records — customers, the curious, and strangers. The second is the risk of absence: a delivery of legal papers does not wait for you to get back. If the server arrives on a day you are traveling, working away, or simply not answering the door, the case moves forward without you, and a judge can rule against your company without you saying a single word — what is called a default judgment. Neither cost arrives on an invoice; both arrive on the wrong day.

When you need a service — and what it costs

There is one case with no choice at all: if you formed your LLC in a state where you do not live, you need an agent in that state — a person or company with a physical address there. The same applies if your company registers in a second state: each state requires its own agent. And there are cases where paying is simply the sensible decision: you travel a lot, you work away from home all day, or you do not want your personal address on a public record. A commercial registered-agent service runs between $50 and $300 a year depending on the company (published prices checked in November 2026 run from about $50 at the low end to $249–$299 at the high end). What that buys is not a piece of paper: it is an address that is not your home, and someone present during business hours, every business day.

What happens if you stop having an agent?

Maintaining an agent is not optional, and letting it lapse has a chain of consequences. With no agent on file, your LLC first loses its good standing with the state; if the gap continues — in many states the process can begin after 60 days without an agent — the state can dissolve your company administratively, without asking you. And in the meantime the papers risk continues: a lawsuit delivered to an old or empty address does not stop; the case proceeds and the default judgment lands anyway. Changing agents, or renewing a service, is a minor filing compared with reviving a dissolved company.

Frequently asked questions

What is a registered agent, and can I be my own?

A registered agent is the person or company whose physical address the state and the courts use to deliver legal papers to your LLC — lawsuits and official notices. Every LLC must have one in the state where it was formed. Yes, you can be your own if you live in that state, have a real physical address there (not a P.O. box), and are at that address during business hours to receive papers in person.

Why is my registered agent's address public?

Because the agent registry exists so that anyone — the state, a court, someone suing your company — can officially locate your LLC. Your agent's name and physical street address are public record, open to whoever looks them up. If you are your own agent with your home address, that is the address that stays exposed.

What happens if lawsuit papers arrive and I am not there?

The case does not stop. Delivery is made to the registered address and the process moves on; if your company does not respond, a judge can rule against it without it presenting a defense — a default judgment. That is the central risk of being your own agent when you are not at that address during business hours.

How much does a registered agent service cost, and when do I need one?

A commercial service runs between $50 and $300 a year depending on the company. You are required to have one if you formed your LLC in a state where you do not live, because the agent must have a physical address in that state. And it is worth paying for if you travel a lot, work away from home all day, or do not want your personal address on public record.
